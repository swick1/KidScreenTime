Kid Screen Time — How It Helps
================================

Screens aren't the enemy. Used without any structure, though, they tend to create the same
three problems in kids: too much undifferentiated time in front of them, not enough time away
from them, and no practice handling the frustration games are specifically designed to
provoke. Kid Screen Time is built around those three problems directly, not around generic
"blocking" for its own sake.


1. Too much screen time, whenever the kid wants it
----------------------------------------------------
The core of the app is a weekly schedule: a parent picks which blocks of time each day the
computer is usable at all. Outside those blocks, the screen locks — no negotiation, no "just
five more minutes," because the app isn't the one saying no, the calendar is.

For families who care less about *when* and more about *how much*, Total Screen Time mode adds
a second, stricter layer on top of the calendar: a daily cap on actual active time (e.g. 2
hours), regardless of how wide the calendar window is. It only counts real activity — mouse
and keyboard input — so a paused video or an idle, forgotten session doesn't quietly burn the
budget. Once the cap is hit, the device locks for the day even if the calendar would otherwise
allow more, and doesn't reopen until midnight.

Warning sounds and toasts at 10 minutes, 5 minutes, 1 minute, and 10 seconds before a lock give
the kid a real chance to save progress and wrap up, rather than getting cut off mid-task.


2. Not enough time away from the screen
------------------------------------------
Reducing total time in front of a screen is only half the goal — the other half is making sure
some of that reclaimed time actually turns into something else: moving, reading, being bored
in a productive way.

When a session ends without incident (see below), the app doesn't just say "nice job" — it
shows a short list of concrete ideas for the break: go for a walk, get some water, read a few
pages, shoot some hoops, draw something, listen to music. It's a nudge, not a mandate, but it
puts a physical or offline alternative in front of the kid at exactly the moment they'd
otherwise just sit there.

The schedule design itself pushes the same direction: a parent can carve out deliberate gaps
between sessions (a lunch break, an evening cutoff) so screen time is naturally interspersed
with everything else in the day, instead of being one unbroken block.


3. Learning to handle frustration, not just avoiding it
------------------------------------------------------------
This is the part most screen time tools skip entirely. Losing, dying, getting stuck — these
are normal parts of playing anything worth playing, and getting upset about it isn't the
problem. The problem is when frustration turns into yelling, banging on the desk, or a full
meltdown, with no one there to help the kid notice it happening.

Kid Screen Time listens for that specific pattern — sudden, sharp bursts of loud noise, not
just background chatter or a loud game — using the microphone, entirely on-device. It's
deliberately picky about what counts:
  - It adapts to the room's normal background noise, so a noisy house or a running AC unit
    doesn't trip it.
  - It cross-checks against the computer's own audio output, so a loud sound effect or gunshot
    in a game doesn't get mistaken for a real outburst.
  - It filters out the keyboard itself, so a loud mechanical keyboard or rapid-fire clicking
    during an intense moment (a rhythm game, a boss fight) doesn't get miscounted either.

A single loud moment doesn't do anything — everybody has one of those. Only a real pattern of
repeated outbursts within a short window triggers a short, mandatory break, framed as exactly
that: a break, not a punishment. No lecture, no shame — just a calm screen, a rotating
supportive message, a countdown, and the same list of things to go do instead.

The goal isn't to prevent frustration. It's to give a kid a beat to notice it, step away, and
come back having practiced doing that themselves — which is the actual skill, not "never
getting upset in the first place."


4. Reinforcing the behavior you want, not just the one you don't
------------------------------------------------------------------
Every one of these systems is easy to build as pure punishment: lock the screen, end the
session, subtract time. Kid Screen Time also rewards the alternative. A session that ends
clean — no outbursts at all — earns a small bonus block of extra time and a short pop-up
celebrating it. A brief, encouraging recap (via Pixi, the app's mascot) shows up on the lock
screen afterward too, matched to how the session actually went: proud when it went well,
gentle and encouraging when it didn't.

The point of all of it is the same: not to keep a kid off the computer, but to help them build
a real, lasting relationship with it — one where time is bounded, breaks happen on purpose, and
losing your temper is something you notice and recover from, not something that just happens
to you.
